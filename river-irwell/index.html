A Mancunianity Flows public exhibition

The River Carries Water. People Carry Stories.

Explore the River Irwell through movement, memory, resilience, wildlife and belonging.

Written and researched by Nakib Narat.

A river can divide two cities and connect them at the same time.

Follow the Irwell through boats, railways, horses, fire, floods and environmental recovery, then imagine a future rich in wildlife, learning, accessible leisure and new stories.

Illustrated edition in development. Six original interpretive illustrations are being developed for a later edition. This complete text-first edition contains no empty image frames or unfinished artwork.

The story in two minutes

The River Irwell rises on moorland north of Bacup and travels through countryside, former mill towns and neighbourhoods before reaching Manchester and Salford. Its lower course becomes part of the Manchester Ship Canal system.

People have used the Irwell for water, fishing, travel, trade, industry and recreation. They built bridges over it, warehouses beside it and railways above it. Industry brought work, skill, design, invention and wealth, but it also brought severe pollution and exploitation. Floods have repeatedly tested riverside communities. Environmental improvements have helped wildlife return, while sewage, litter, invasive plants, hard riverbanks and climate pressures remain.

Near Aviva Studios, three forms of movement can be understood together: water below, footsteps across and trains above. The cargo changed. Connection remained.

Mancunianity Flows follows the river as both a physical route and a human one. Its Weavers, older people with connections to Manchester and Salford, explore how work, family history, neighbourhoods, journeys and environmental change give places meaning.

Why I created Mancunianity Flows

Mancunianity Flows grew from my interest in the relationship between people and place, and from my voluntary work with older people across Manchester and Greater Manchester.

I am Chair of the Greater Manchester Older People's Equality Panel and a member of the Age Friendly Manchester Older People's Board. This work has shown me that resilience is experienced in ordinary life. It matters whether somebody can understand a warning, use public transport, find somewhere to rest or reach a green or blue space without unnecessary barriers.

Older people should not enter these conversations only as people who may need support. They bring knowledge gathered over decades. Some remember polluted waterways, earlier floods, lost buildings, changing neighbourhoods, former transport systems and the gradual return of wildlife.

The project uses walking, history, sound and creative exploration to ask what records can tell us, what lived experience adds and how both can help us think about the future of the Irwell.

Every river has more than one story

The Irwell has carried water through this landscape for thousands of years. People have asked it to carry much more. It has carried boats, goods, industrial waste and floodwater. Horses worked beside it. Trains crossed above it. Families lived, worked and travelled nearby.

The project is principally focused on the central Irwell corridor around Manchester and Salford, including River Square, Water Street and Liverpool Road. That focus is not intended to reduce Salford to the area facing Manchester city centre, or to overlook the wider river valley through Broughton, Pendleton, Clifton, Swinton, Weaste and Eccles.

The historic township, later borough and city, present City of Salford and wider Irwell catchment are different geographies. The river also interacts with present-day Trafford and was altered as sections were straightened, dredged, redirected and absorbed into the Manchester Ship Canal system.

From River Square

Stand near River Square beside Aviva Studios and look upwards as well as across. The Irwell moves below. People cross between Manchester and Salford. Above them, trains travel on the Ordsall Chord.

Aviva Studios occupies a landscape shaped by water, railway bridges, warehouses and former freight activity. Long before audiences gathered here for art and performance, goods passed through nearby yards, arches, quays and streets.

The Irwell changes with the weather. Overcast sky can turn the water pewter grey. Rain deepens the colour of brick and leaves reflections trembling beneath bridges. A train may arrive first as a low vibration, grow into a roar, then disappear in seconds.

Sound behaves differently under an arch or bridge. Footsteps become sharper. Water echoes against hard walls. Mancunianity Flows uses listening as well as looking because a landscape can reveal itself through noise, rhythm and silence.

A river begins in the rain

A river does not begin only at its spring. Rain falls onto hills, fields, roofs, roads, gardens and pavements. Some soaks into soil. Some enters drains and streams. Some moves rapidly over hard surfaces towards larger watercourses. A raindrop landing many miles away may eventually affect communities beside the lower Irwell.

The river's name is much older than modern Manchester. Different explanations connect it with early words for water, springs or pale water, but the precise origin remains uncertain. Names travel through time rather like rivers. Their beginnings are not always easy to locate.

The human river

No two people meet the same river in exactly the same way. For one person, the Irwell may be a boundary crossed on the way to work. For another, it is the view from a flat, a place to fish, a childhood route, a flood warning or a walk once shared with somebody important.

A warehouse record can tell us what arrived. A family story may reveal what happened next. A map can show where a bridge stood. A memory can explain why crossing it mattered.

The Weavers will arrive with different maps in their heads. One person may remember a street before redevelopment. Another may know where a relative worked, where buses once stopped or which bridge mattered on the journey into town. This edition contains no invented Weaver testimony. Real contributions can be added later, after participants have developed them and approved their public use.

Boats before trains

Long before locomotives crossed this landscape, boats and barges carried goods. The lower Irwell became part of the Mersey and Irwell Navigation, connecting Manchester with the Mersey and the port of Liverpool.

At Water Street, goods moved between boats, warehouses, roads and workplaces. This required boat crews, warehouse workers, carters, handlers, horses and people keeping routes and locks working.

The landscape still offers clues. Blocked openings, changes in brick colour, ramps, retaining walls and buildings that appear to face the wrong way may reveal vanished quays, sidings, yards and working routes.

The railway arrives

On 15 September 1830, the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened, with Liverpool Road as its Manchester terminus. It connected Liverpool's port with industrial Manchester and carried passengers as well as goods.

Raw cotton travelled towards Manchester's mills, while finished textiles moved back towards Liverpool and international markets. Much of the cotton arriving from the United States had been grown and picked by enslaved people. The railway was an extraordinary technological achievement, but the prosperity it supported was connected with exploitation far beyond Manchester.

Calico printing opens another history of the Irwell Valley through pattern, colour, chemistry, design and skilled work. It was lucrative and highly polluting. The knowledge behind printed cottons also leads to Britain's colonisation of India, reminding us that local innovation and global power cannot be separated neatly.

Railways also changed people's relationship with time. Shared timetables made small differences between local clocks increasingly difficult. Liverpool Road is associated with an early station bell and a sundial. What happens on a dark and rainy Manchester morning?

Iron horses still needed real horses

Steam locomotives transformed travel between cities, but they did not carry every crate to its final destination. Horses pulled carts through railway yards and streets, moving goods between wagons, warehouses, factories, shops and homes.

Wagons could enter the 1830 Warehouse at an upper level. Goods then moved between floors using hoists and continued their journeys by road.

Archaeological investigations found oyster and cockle shells at Liverpool Road, a vivid clue to foods passing through the station. The meal disappeared long ago. The shells survived.

A pineapple too precious to eat?

The elevated railway across part of Liverpool Road became known as the Pineapple Line. The Science and Industry Museum connects the name with the Georgian-period Pineapple Inn, which stood beneath the viaduct on Water Street until 1986.

Pineapples were rare and expensive in Georgian Britain. They became symbols of wealth, hospitality and fashionable taste, and were sometimes displayed or hired rather than eaten. What object might somebody display today to create the same effect?

Fire and the pig escape

At about 1am on 23 May 1866, fire broke out in No. 2 Warehouse at Liverpool Road Station. The building contained turpentine, oil, soap, soda ash and other flammable materials. A strong wind fed the blaze, which spread into No. 1 Warehouse.

Fire brigades from Manchester, Salford and surrounding places responded. Two warehouses were gutted, but crews prevented the flames crossing into the surviving 1830 Warehouse.

Liverpool Road also had a pig landing station near Charles Street, now Grape Street. Newspaper accounts described workers trying to move pigs out of the yard as the fire raged. The story may raise a smile today, but the situation was dangerous.

The river rises

The flood lines on the Peel Park obelisk belong to the 19th century, but flooding is not a closed chapter. In November 1866, the Irwell flooded Lower Broughton and Peel Park. Three men died, and residents became trapped upstairs in houses.

The monument turns measurement into memory. It asks each generation to look up and imagine the river occupying ordinary human space.

On Boxing Day 2015, Storm Eva brought exceptional rainfall to Greater Manchester. More than 2,250 homes and 500 businesses were flooded across the city region. The clean-up involved emergency services, councils, housing staff, volunteers and neighbours.

Climate resilience and slowing the water

Climate change does not cause every individual flood. Flooding depends on rainfall, previous weather, soil, drains, buildings, roads, river levels, defences and the shape of the land. Climate change alters the conditions in which those factors interact.

Resilience means preparing, coping, recovering and adapting. The same flood does not affect everybody equally. Health, disability, income, housing, transport, language and social support all matter. A flood map shows where water may travel. A community remembers what happened when it arrived.

Flood walls, embankments and storage basins protect homes and businesses, but no defence can remove every risk. Natural flood management uses trees, hedges, healthier soils, wetlands, ponds, floodplains and carefully designed leaky barriers to slow rainwater before it reaches densely populated areas.

A river used as a drain

Industrial Manchester and Salford treated the Irwell as part of their machinery. Factories, workshops, mines, sewers and heavily populated neighbourhoods sent waste into the river and its tributaries. Some stretches became unable to support the fish and wildlife that had once lived there.

Later regulation, sewage treatment, clean-up projects and habitat work contributed to improvement. Recovery remains incomplete. Sewage discharges, litter, invasive plants, fragmented habitat and hard-engineered banks continue to affect the catchment.

A fish needs more than water. It needs oxygen, food, shelter, suitable places to breed and a route through the river. Birds depend on fish, insects, plants and undisturbed places to rest.

Wildlife and plants

Wildlife changes with the season, weather, water level and location. A grey wagtail may bob beside fast-moving water. A heron may stand almost motionless. A kingfisher may appear as a brief blue flash. Cormorants dive for fish, while mallards, moorhens and coots favour quieter water.

A quiet visit with no sightings is not a failed visit. Weather, season, water level, time of day and disturbance all affect what appears.

Trees and shrubs provide shade and nesting places. Himalayan balsam can crowd out native vegetation. Japanese knotweed spreads through small fragments. Giant hogweed can cause serious skin reactions. Remain on a safe public path and do not handle unknown plants.

The Weavers

The Weavers bring human experience into the story of environmental change. Some may remember the Irwell when industrial pollution was more visible. Others may remember floods, changing neighbourhoods, difficult paths or the return of birds and fish.

Their memories do not replace scientific evidence. They help explain how change was experienced. A gauge records a flood peak. A resident remembers the electricity going off, a neighbour at the door or the months spent repairing a home.

Through conversation, mapping, walking, drawing and sound, the Weavers connect the river's past with present risks and future choices. No Weaver's words, image or recording will be made public without clear approval.

Beyond one image of Salford

The Irwell's industrial history must acknowledge poverty, exploitation and environmental damage, but Salford should not be reduced to a permanent sequence of smoke, tin baths, cobbles and passive suffering.

The wider story also contains ingenuity, design, skilled labour, education, aspiration, organisation, cultural confidence and national influence. People crossed the Irwell daily for work, leisure, worship, trade and family life. Manchester and Salford remained distinct, but their populations, economies and cultures were inseparably connected.

Ewan MacColl's Dirty Old Town offers an important artistic portrait of Salford and its industrial landscape. Within Mancunianity Flows it can be treated as a doorway into discussion, not the final verdict on a city.

The river we could live with

For much of its modern history, people asked what the Irwell could carry away. Its future raises a better question: what could the river bring into everyday life?

It could bring children closer to wildlife. It could offer an older resident a short, level walk with somewhere to rest. It could connect Manchester and Salford through useful and attractive routes. It could give anglers a healthier fishery, schools an outdoor classroom and communities places to meet, learn and breathe.

CyanLines proposes a long-term network linking parks, canals, rivers, squares and other green and blue spaces. The Irwell River Park strategy shares ambitions for healthier lives, active travel, accessibility, climate resilience, nature and stronger connections with the river corridor's history and culture.

A route is not genuinely accessible simply because it appears on a map. It needs maintained surfaces, step-free alternatives, seating, shade, shelter, accessible toilets, safe crossings, clear information, shorter choices and public-transport connections.

The Irwell can carry sound and stories alongside water. Audio walks, oral histories, poetry, photography, school artwork, guided walks, wildlife recording and approved Weaver stories can help people notice what is already there.

What should the Irwell become next?

The river's future will be shaped by decisions about land, homes, transport, sewage, flood protection, public space, wildlife, culture and investment.

A more resilient Irwell could have cleaner water, fewer damaging discharges, connected habitats, natural flood management upstream, maintained defences and understandable warnings. A more welcoming Irwell could provide accessible paths, seats, shade, toilets and quiet places.

These futures will sometimes compete. Wildlife needs quiet. Popular routes need maintenance. Flood protection may require hard engineering. New development should improve the river rather than use it simply as scenery.

Mancunianity Flows helps people meet the Irwell as a real place rather than an abstract problem. Science, engineering, regulation and investment will shape what happens next. So will the attention people give the river.

What we hope will remain

The live walks will end, but the work should continue to flow. Mancunianity Flows aims to leave a permanent digital exhibition, six original illustrations, accessible family activities, carefully researched local history and a safe way to add approved Weaver contributions.

The project will have succeeded if people notice more, ask better questions and recognise that everyday journeys and family memories belong within public history.

The project does not seek to finish the River Irwell's story. It invites more people to enter it.

River Detective challenges

Follow a raindrop

Choose a roof or paved surface. Trace where rainfall may travel through gutters, drains, slopes, soil and planted areas.

Find a working clue

Look for an arch, blocked opening, loading bay, retaining wall, ramp or sudden change in ground level. What earlier activity might explain its shape?

Listen to an arch

Compare how footsteps, voices, trains and water sound in an open space and beneath a bridge.

Test a resilient route

Imagine the path after heavy rain or during a heatwave. Does it have safe exits, shade, seating, clear information and step-free alternatives?

Create a memory map

With permission, ask an older person about a flood, polluted waterway, journey or environmental change. Mark the place and record what changed.

Design one improvement

Choose one realistic addition to the river corridor. Who would benefit? How would wildlife be protected? What maintenance would it need?
