Our purpose

Accessible, accurate and useful beyond the live programme

Mancunianity Flows is creating an accessible and growing public resource about the River Irwell, Manchester, Salford and the people, environments and systems that shaped them. Researched history is brought into conversation with lived experience to support family discovery, school learning, community participation and reflection about the river's future.

Accurate

We distinguish established facts, interpretation and questions still being investigated.

Accessible

We design for different ages, devices, senses, bodies and ways of learning.

Participatory

Local lived experience helps shape the questions, stories and resources.

Ethical

Permission, safeguarding, privacy and respectful representation guide publication.

Reusable

Resources are easy to find, print and use where rights permit.

Sustainable

Material is reviewed, backed up, evaluated and maintained beyond the live walks.

Stories from the River

Historical doorways, with the live surprises still inside

The Irwell carries stories of invention, work, movement, animals, danger, pollution, resilience and change. These short tasters introduce the history without reproducing the promenade performance. The live experience uses place, sound, music, humour and participation to reveal the stories differently.

Spoiler-light by design: key dramatic turns, comic payoffs, exact stopping points, musical transitions and the final performance sequence remain part of the live walk.
Story taster

The Great Convergence

Beside the Irwell, several ages of movement occupy one compact landscape. Water, railway engineering, roads, bridges and walking routes did not simply replace one another. They accumulated, moving people, animals, goods, money and ideas between Manchester and Salford.

Look closely: Find one route over the river, one beside it and one following the water.

The exact revealing order, viewpoint and sound sequence remain part of the live experience.

Story taster

From Industrial Site to Cultural Factory

The landscape around Aviva Studios has repeatedly been a place of production. Railway and industrial activity gave way to television production associated with Granada. Today, performances, music, exhibitions and shared cultural experiences are made here.

Discuss: Is this a break from the industrial past, or another kind of factory?

The walk connects this visible landscape to local memories and the next historical clue.

Story taster

The Diligent Spider

In 1844, French observer Léon Faucher pictured Manchester as a diligent spider at the centre of a vast web. Roads and railways linked labour, raw materials, manufacturers and markets, but the image also raises a harder question: did the web connect places equally, or draw wealth and power towards its centre?

Fact, clue or interpretation? Faucher's metaphor is historical evidence, but it remains one observer's view.

The full inscription, audience responses and theatrical transition are held back for the promenade.

Story taster

The Railway City Still Needed Horses

Steam transformed long-distance travel, but the railway city still relied on working animals. Horses moved carts, worked in depots and entered spaces where locomotives could not. Food, water, shelter and waste all formed part of transport's hidden infrastructure.

Investigate: What might pierced brickwork and concealed railway spaces have ventilated, protected or hidden?

The physical clue, hoof rhythm and answer are revealed on the walk.

Story taster

Small Clues, Busy City

Bees and a globe speak of industry and worldwide connections. A jostling stone protected a vulnerable corner from iron-rimmed wheels. Marks left by a lost bridge ask how vanished infrastructure should be remembered. Small details can expose the pressures of an intensely busy city.

Look closely: Find one surviving feature designed to protect a building from moving traffic.

Historical care: popular interpretations are labelled as interpretations when the original reason is not settled.

Story taster

The Fire and the Pig Escape

On 23 May 1866, two cotton warehouses burned within the railway freight landscape. Livestock also arrived by rail to feed the expanding industrial city. During the fire, pigs in the station landscape created noise, confusion and a considerable practical challenge.

Consider: Why are animals often missing from familiar accounts of the railway city?

Historical care: the often-quoted figure of 80,000 pigs refers to annual traffic, not animals present during the fire. The escape and comic payoff remain live.

Story taster

Margaret Fletcher and a Lost Canal

The Manchester, Bolton and Bury Canal once joined the Irwell in this landscape. Former nurse Margaret Fletcher chaired the canal's restoration society for nearly 20 years. The achievement was sustained campaigning, leadership and partnership, making a neglected waterway visible again.

Discuss: What local place would your group protect or bring back into public view?

The remaining traces and transition into the finale are part of the live journey.

Brief glimpse

Railways Changed Time

Liverpool Road opened on 15 September 1830 as the Manchester terminus of the pioneering inter-urban railway. Faster journeys made slightly different local town times increasingly impractical.

Question: What happens to daily life when journeys become faster than local clocks can comfortably accommodate?

The audience timetable question, sundial and full interpretive reveal remain part of the live performance.

Brief glimpse

The River That Carried the Cost

The Irwell supported industrial growth and absorbed its consequences. Dyeing, bleaching, drains and sewage severely damaged the waterway. Conditions have improved, but pollution, habitat pressure and flood risk remain part of an unfinished recovery.

Investigate: Look for evidence of flood management, wildlife and changing public access.

The sound treatment and movement from damage to recovery remain live.

Brief glimpse

The Inland Port

Manchester was inland, but civic ambition sought direct connection with the sea. The 36-mile Manchester Ship Canal created the Port of Manchester, while much of the associated dock landscape stood physically in Salford.

Consider: What does that apparent contradiction reveal about the relationship between the two cities?

The destination puzzle, musical response and finale are deliberately not disclosed online.

A living river

Environment, flooding and the future city

The Irwell’s environmental story is not a simple move from a damaged past to a perfect present. The river connects industrial pollution and ecological recovery with continuing questions about extreme rainfall, flood risk, biodiversity, access and resilience.

On Boxing Day 2015, around 750 properties were flooded internally in Lower Broughton. At the Manchester Racecourse gauge, the Irwell reached 5.67 metres, approximately 1.3 metres higher than any previous reading in 75 years.

Use the river to ask: How should cities live safely beside water? Where can wildlife thrive? Who has access to green and blue spaces? What should future generations inherit?

Source: Greater Manchester Boxing Day 2015 Flood Investigation Report.

Connected blue and green spaces

Learning from CyanLines

Mancunianity Flows shares CyanLines’ belief that rivers, canals, parks, waterfronts and walking routes should be celebrated, protected and made easier to discover.

CyanLines is developing a vision for more than 100 miles of connected parks, green corridors, waterfronts and traffic-free walking and wheeling routes across Manchester, Salford and the wider city region.

We are delighted that Mancunianity Flows will be shared through CyanLines’ public communications and newsletters.
