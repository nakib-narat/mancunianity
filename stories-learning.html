Stories from the river

Historical doorways, with the live surprises still inside

These short tasters introduce the landscape without reproducing the promenade performance. The live event reveals the stories through place, wireless sound, music, humour and participation.

Spoiler-light by design: exact stopping points, theatrical turns, musical transitions and the final performance sequence remain part of the live walk.
Story taster

The Great Convergence

Several ages of movement occupy one compact landscape. River, railway, roads, bridges and walking routes accumulated rather than simply replacing one another.

Look closely: find one route over the river, one beside it and one following the water.

Story taster

The Diligent Spider

In 1844, French observer Léon Faucher pictured Manchester as a diligent spider at the centre of a vast web. The image described connections between labour, raw materials, manufacturers and markets, while also raising questions about where power and wealth gathered.

Think: did the web connect places equally, or draw resources towards its centre?

Story taster

The Railway City Still Needed Horses

Steam transformed long journeys, but working animals still moved carts, served depots and entered places locomotives could not. Food, water, shelter and waste were part of transport's hidden infrastructure.

Investigate: what might concealed railway spaces have protected or housed?

Story taster

The Fire and the Pig Escape

On 23 May 1866, two cotton warehouses burned within the railway freight landscape. Livestock also arrived by rail to feed the growing city, placing animals inside this complex industrial system.

Consider: why are animals often missing from familiar accounts of the railway city?

Historical care: the often-quoted figure of 80,000 pigs refers to annual traffic, not the number present during the fire.

Story taster

Liverpool Road and a New Railway Age

The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened on 15 September 1830. Liverpool Road was its Manchester terminus, carrying passengers and goods and helping establish a new model for railway travel.

Ask: how did faster movement change work, trade and daily life?

Source: Science and Industry Museum.

Story taster

The River That Carried the Cost

The Irwell supported industrial growth and absorbed its consequences. In 1950, Anthony Greenwood called it the hardest-working river in the United Kingdom and described more than 100 cotton mills along the Irwell and Roch, alongside many other industries.

Investigate: look for signs of damage, recovery, wildlife and flood management.

Source: UK Parliament, 18 April 1950.

Story taster

Margaret Fletcher and a Lost Canal

The Manchester, Bolton and Bury Canal once joined the Irwell here. Margaret Fletcher chaired the canal's restoration society for nearly 20 years, helping return a neglected waterway to public attention.

Discuss: what local place would your group protect or bring back into view?

Story taster

The Inland Port

Manchester was inland, but the Ship Canal created a direct connection with the sea. Much of the dock landscape stood physically in Salford, revealing the close and sometimes complicated relationship between the two cities.

Consider: what does the Port of Manchester name tell us about identity and power?

A living river

Environment, flooding and the future city

The Irwell's environmental story is not a simple move from a damaged past to a perfect present. Pollution, ecological recovery, extreme rainfall, flood risk, biodiversity and public access remain connected.

On Boxing Day 2015, around 750 properties were flooded internally in Lower Broughton. The Manchester Racecourse gauge reached 5.67 metres, about 1.3 metres above any previous reading in its 75-year record.

Use the river to ask: how should cities live safely beside water? Where can wildlife thrive? Who can reach green and blue spaces? What should future generations inherit?

Source: Greater Manchester Boxing Day 2015 Flood Investigation Report.

Connected blue and green spaces

Learning from CyanLines

CyanLines aims to create more than 100 miles of routes across Manchester and Salford, then reach across Greater Manchester. Its first pilot routes include an Irwell and Castlefield loop.

The project brings parks, waterways, green corridors and walking and wheeling routes into one connected vision.
